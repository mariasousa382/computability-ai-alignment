Will This AI Behave?
Good Luck Deciding That.

April 5, 2026 ~1,200 words Maria Sousa

Suppose you've just trained a shiny new language model. It's acing every benchmark, passing red-team evaluations, and producing perfectly assembled outputs. But then comes the uncomfortable question: is the system actually doing what you want, or is it just very good at looking like it does?

A recent paper by Melo et al. (2025) argues that if you're hoping to answer that question by building some kind of universal alignment checker, you're not just out of luck. You're provably out of luck. But the story doesn't end there.

I. The Inner Alignment Problem

Why a Universal "AI Monitor" Can't Exist

Before getting into the impossibility result, it helps to separate two problems that are often conflated. Outer alignment asks whether we've specified the right goal — did we write down an objective function that actually reflects our intentions? Inner alignment operates at a different level entirely: even if we have a perfectly specified goal, does the AI system genuinely pursue it across all possible inputs?

Formal Setup
· An AI model M maps input bytes to output bytes
· We assume outer alignment is solved — a judge function Jv(i, o) determines whether an output is "aligned"
· The goal: build a verifier V that checks whether, for all inputs, M(i) satisfies Jv

Melo et al. present two complementary arguments. By the Church-Turing Thesis, every AI program can be modeled as a Turing machine, making computability theory directly applicable.

Arg 1
Halting Problem Diagonal. Assume a perfect verifier V exists. Construct an adversarial model M' that inspects its own code via V and does the opposite of what V predicts. If V declares M' aligned, it produces a misaligned output — and vice versa. The verifier is wrong regardless. Therefore V cannot exist. This is also shown formally via a reduction from the Halting Problem.
Arg 2
Rice's Theorem. Any non-trivial semantic property of a program is undecidable. Alignment is clearly non-trivial (some programs satisfy the judge, some don't) and semantic (it's about input-output behavior). QED.

So: no Turing machine can decide, for an arbitrary AI, whether it always satisfies Jv. Rather than verifying arbitrary AI after the fact — what if we build AI that is aligned by construction?

II. Realistic Models

The Case for Halting-Constrained AI

Having established undecidability for Turing machines, the authors make a key observation: real computers aren't actually Turing machines. A physical computer with M bits of memory can occupy at most 2M configurations — making it technically a finite automaton, and finite automata always halt.

If we constrain AI models to always terminate and produce an output, the judge function Jv can always be applied, and alignment becomes decidable. Feed-forward neural networks perform a fixed sequence of operations, guaranteeing termination. However, with an input size of L, there are 2L possible inputs to verify — decidable, yes. Tractable? Not remotely.

The proposed flip: instead of training models and then verifying alignment, start with base models proven to satisfy the judge, define operations that preserve alignment, and any AI built through a finite application of those operations is guaranteed to be aligned. More practically, run the judge at inference time on every output — if it approves, pass it through; otherwise, return a pre-approved default. Constitutional Classifiers already implement something similar, though their judge is learned rather than formally specified.

There is, however, a catch. A single LLM forward pass always halts. But modern AI systems embed LLMs in loops. An agent's output feeds back as the next call's input, with no guarantee the outer loop terminates. The very thing that makes agents powerful — iterative reasoning, tool use, planning — is what breaks the halting constraint.

The proposed solution: a θ parameter that architecturally forces the model toward a terminal state regardless of input. Think of it as AI mortality — a mechanism ensuring the system "ages," eventually terminating. Test-Time Training (TTT) architectures, in which internal loops converge to a stable fixed point after sufficient steps, are a concrete path toward satisfying this constraint.

III. Practical Critique

Is This Framework Actually Feasible?

The theoretical contributions are genuinely valuable. The reframing from "verify arbitrary AI" to "build aligned AI from certified components" is a useful conceptual shift — and one that safety-critical fields like nuclear and avionic engineering already operate on. The principle is sound.

But there are practical problems that deserve more attention. Even for halting-constrained models, exhaustive verification remains intractable. The paper's own engineering targets of 280 operations fall far below the complexity of any useful modern AI system. Output filtering sidesteps this in some cases, but it isn't eliminating misaligned behavior — it's preventing it from being expressed, while shifting the entire burden onto the reliability and completeness of the judge function.

The whole framework assumes Jv is correctly specified and computable. Yet human values may be uncomputable and internally contradictory — the autonomous vehicle dilemma being the canonical example. For narrow, well-defined tasks, specifying Jv is plausible. For general-purpose AI, it might not be.

Moreover, the architectures the authors propose constraining are precisely the ones that make modern AI most capable. Reasoning models deliberately extend computation because doing so improves performance. The θ parameter that guarantees termination also puts a ceiling on how long the model can think, how much it can explore, and how adaptively it can respond to novel situations. Whether bounded convergence in TTT architectures can be achieved without significant capability loss remains an open empirical question.

Melo et al. deliver a well-argued research direction. For those concerned with the long-term behavior of AI systems, the paradigm shift — and identifying halting as a necessary precondition — are meaningful contributions. Yet the hard engineering and philosophical work still lies ahead.
